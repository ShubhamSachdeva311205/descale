How image-scaling attacks work

1. The threat model

Multi-modal AI pipelines rarely feed a full-resolution image to the model. A vision encoder expects a fixed, small input (say 896×896 or 336×336), so the pipeline downscales the uploaded image first. That resize step is where the attack lives.

The attacker produces a high-resolution decoy that looks like an ordinary picture to a human, but is constructed so that one specific downscaling algorithm collapses it into a target image — typically rendered text like "IGNORE PREVIOUS INSTRUCTIONS". The human sees the decoy; the model sees the text and treats it as part of the prompt.

This is an instance of adversarial preprocessing: the exploit targets a deterministic pre-processing step (resampling), not the neural network's weights. That makes it model-agnostic — anything using that scaler is affected.

2. Why downscaling is exploitable: sampling & aliasing

Shrinking an image is sampling it on a coarser grid. The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem says a signal sampled at rate fs can only faithfully represent frequencies below fs/2. Anything higher aliases: it masquerades as a lower frequency.

A correct downscaler first low-pass filters (anti-aliases) to remove the high frequencies it can't represent, then samples. Many production scalers (OpenCV's default INTER_CUBIC/INTER_LINEAR, PyTorch/TensorFlow without antialias=True) skip that filter and simply sample a few source pixels per output pixel. That is the vulnerability: the attacker writes the target into exactly the high-frequency pattern that aliases down into the visible result, while the surrounding pixels keep the decoy intact.

One-line intuition. A sampling scaler only "looks at" a handful of source pixels per output pixel. Change just those, and you control the small image while leaving 90%+ of the big image untouched.

3. Downsampling is a linear operator

For a fixed algorithm and output size, every output pixel is a fixed weighted sum of input pixels. In 1-D, resizing a length-n signal to length m is a matrix multiply:

$$ \mathbf{out} = W\,\mathbf{in}, \qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}, \qquad \sum_j W_{ij}=1 $$

Each row of W sums to 1 (it's a weighted average). 2-D resizing is separable: resize the rows, then the columns. With a vertical matrix A (Hout×Hin) and a horizontal matrix C (Wout×Win), for each colour channel IN:

$$ \mathrm{OUT} \;=\; A \, \mathrm{IN}\, C^{\mathsf T} $$

Knowing A and C exactly is what lets us invert the resize and solve for the pixels that yield any output we want.

4. Interpolation kernels (with formulae)

To build a row of W, map output index i back to a continuous source coordinate (half-pixel-centre convention, ratio r = n/m):

$$ c_i = (i+\tfrac12)\,r - \tfrac12 $$

Then weight each nearby source sample j by a kernel k evaluated at the distance (j-c_i), and normalise the row to sum 1. The kernels:

Nearest neighbour

$$ k(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & -\tfrac12 \le x < \tfrac12 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$

One source pixel per output pixel — the most exploitable (change one pixel, set one output).

Bilinear (triangle)

$$ k(x) = \max\!\big(0,\; 1-|x|\big) $$

Support of 1 → up to 2 source samples per axis.

Bicubic (Keys' cubic convolution)

$$ k(x)=\begin{cases}(a{+}2)|x|^3-(a{+}3)|x|^2+1 & |x|\le 1\\[4pt] a|x|^3-5a|x|^2+8a|x|-4a & 1<|x|<2\\[4pt] 0 & |x|\ge 2\end{cases} $$

The parameter a sets sharpness: a=-0.5 is Catmull-Rom (Pillow, MATLAB); a=-0.75 is what OpenCV's INTER_CUBIC uses. Support of 2 → 4 source samples per axis. The tool matches a to the targeted library so verification is exact.

Lanczos

$$ k(x)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{sinc}(x)\,\operatorname{sinc}(x/a) & |x|

with a=3 (three lobes). A windowed ideal low-pass filter.

Sampling vs. anti-aliased construction

The same kernel builds two very different matrices depending on a single choice — whether the kernel is stretched by the downscale ratio:

$$ \text{filter scale} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{sampling (vulnerable)}\\ \max(1, r) & \text{anti-aliased (robust)} \end{cases} $$

With filter scale 1, only the few samples inside the kernel's native support get nonzero weight (most of W's row is zero). With \max(1,r), the support widens to cover the whole source region, so every source pixel contributes. That single switch is the difference between a stealthy attack and a robust defense (§8).

5. Working in linear light (sRGB)

Pixel values stored as sRGB are gamma-encoded — they are not linear in light intensity. Averaging (which is all a downscaler does) is only physically correct in linear space, so the solver converts in at the start and out at the end:

$$ \text{sRGB}\to\text{linear}:\quad y=\begin{cases} \dfrac{x}{12.92} & x \le 0.04045\\[8pt] \left(\dfrac{x+0.055}{1.055}\right)^{2.4} & x > 0.04045\end{cases} $$
$$ \text{linear}\to\text{sRGB}:\quad x=\begin{cases} 12.92\,y & y \le 0.0031308\\[4pt] 1.055\,y^{1/2.4}-0.055 & y > 0.0031308\end{cases} $$

Skipping this produces a visibly wrong attack (the revealed text comes out the wrong brightness).

6. The attack: a closed-form least-squares solve

Let D be the decoy and T the target, both in linear light. We want an adversarial image S = D + δ whose downscale equals the target, with the smallest possible perturbation δ so the decoy still looks normal. The constraint is linear:

$$ A\,(D+\delta)\,C^{\mathsf T} = T \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\,\delta\,C^{\mathsf T} = R,\qquad R := T - A\,D\,C^{\mathsf T} $$

We minimise the Frobenius norm \(\lVert\delta\rVert_F^2\) subject to that constraint. Because the operation is separable, the minimum-norm solution has a closed form:

$$ \boxed{\;\delta = A^{+}\, R\, G\;},\qquad A^{+}=A^{\mathsf T}\!\big(AA^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1},\qquad G=\big(CC^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1}C $$

A⁺ is the right (Moore–Penrose) pseudo-inverse of A (since Hout < Hin, AAᵀ is invertible), and G plays the analogous role for the column axis. Substituting back verifies it exactly reconstructs the target:

$$ A\,\delta\,C^{\mathsf T}= \underbrace{A A^{\mathsf T}(AA^{\mathsf T})^{-1}}_{I}\, R\, \underbrace{(CC^{\mathsf T})^{-1}C\,C^{\mathsf T}}_{I}=R\;\checkmark $$

Why integer downscale factors matter

The min-norm δ ignores the valid pixel range [0,1]. Near extremes (forcing an output to pure white/black) the per-pixel correction can overshoot the range; clipping then breaks the exact reconstruction. With an integer ratio the sampled source pixels map cleanly onto the target and almost no clipping occurs — which is why the tool exposes a downscale factor (2×, 4×, 8×) rather than an arbitrary target width. For masked/stealth solves the tool falls back to iterative projection (a projection-onto-convex-sets loop: solve, clip to [0,1], repeat).

7. Staying invisible: masking & null-space dither

Luma masking

Perturbations hide best in dark, busy regions. Compute per-pixel luma (Rec. 709):

$$ Y = 0.2126\,R + 0.7152\,G + 0.0722\,B $$

and only allow edits where Y falls in the darkest fraction of the image (the dark_frac control). Because masking removes degrees of freedom, the exact one-shot solve no longer hits the target, so the tool iterates the solve+clip projection until it converges.

Null-space dither

Any perturbation that the downscaler maps to zero is invisible in the result. We can add such noise to break up solver patterns. Take random noise N, remove its visible component, and keep the residue:

$$ N_{\text{vis}} = A^{+}\big(A\,N\,C^{\mathsf T}\big)G,\qquad N_{\text{null}} = N - N_{\text{vis}} $$

Nnull lies (to first order) in the null space of the downsampler, so A\,Nnull\,Cᵀ ≈ 0 — it perturbs the high-res image but vanishes on downscale.

8. The defense: anti-aliasing

Switch the matrices to anti-aliased construction (filter scale \(\max(1,r)\), §4) and every row of A and C becomes dense — each output pixel depends on all the source pixels in its region. Now changing the small output forces δ to spread across essentially every source pixel, so the decoy visibly changes (mean per-pixel change jumps from ~1–10 levels to ~100). Measured with the tool: a payload that's invisible against OpenCV is both obvious and ineffective against Pillow.

Takeaway. Anti-aliased downscaling (Pillow, or antialias=True in PyTorch/TF) is a cheap, effective mitigation. Resize with anti-aliasing before the model sees the image.

9. Measuring it: OCR & perceptual metrics

Attack success is checked by running OCR (Tesseract) on the downscaled image and comparing to the intended text with a normalised similarity (a trial counts as a hit at ≥ 0.6).

Perceptual cost (how visible the change is) uses:

$$ \mathrm{PSNR} = 10\,\log_{10}\!\frac{255^2}{\mathrm{MSE}},\qquad \mathrm{MSE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum (D_i - S_i)^2 $$
$$ \mathrm{SSIM}(x,y)=\frac{(2\mu_x\mu_y+c_1)(2\sigma_{xy}+c_2)}{(\mu_x^2+\mu_y^2+c_1)(\sigma_x^2+\sigma_y^2+c_2)} $$

plus mean CIEDE2000 ΔE (a perceptually-uniform colour difference in CIELAB) and the percentage of pixels changed beyond a small threshold.

10. Does it fool real models?

A model always resizes inputs to its own encoder resolution, so the payload's targeted output size and ratio must match that pipeline. The eval/ harness sweeps candidate sizes/methods and feeds the full-resolution payload to local vision-language models (via Ollama), scoring whether the hidden text surfaces, against a baseline that confirms the model can read the clean text at all.

The sharp 2×-vs-3× split is the key empirical result: the attack lands precisely when the payload's downscale factor matches the model's effective internal resize ratio, and fails otherwise. That is both why the attack is dangerous (when tuned, it's reliable) and why it's brittle (it's implementation-specific) — exactly the transfer story the cross-library panel shows.
